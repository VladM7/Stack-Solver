STACK-SOLVER · RESEARCH BRIEF

Realistic Pallet Packing

Warehouse & logistics constraints to make branch-and-price solutions warehouse-ready.

Prepared for the Stack-Solver project · July 4, 2026 · Scope: single- and mixed-SKU palletization for real logistics workflows

This brief surveys warehouse and logistics practice for building pallets — both academic literature on the Distributor's Pallet Packing / mixed-case palletization problem and practitioner guidance from 3PLs, palletizer vendors, and packaging engineers. It then derives a concrete set of realism rules and additional constraints for Stack-Solver, and maps each one onto the existing branch-and-price architecture (pricing-subproblem feasibility vs. objective penalties vs. master constraints), with notes on how each interacts with optimality and branching.

Where this fits

Efficiency (pallet-count minimality via branch-and-price) is already covered. The goal now is a controlled pivot toward realism: pallets that a human or robot can pack, wrap, ship, and unpack safely and quickly. Rules already in the model — the 3-SKU-per-pallet cap, stack height / weight limits, SKU overhang modes, and the load-density top-heavy stacking-order rule — are the baseline this brief extends.

1Executive summary

Across the sources, the same message recurs: a mathematically minimal pallet count is worthless if the resulting pallet cannot be built, wrapped, and unloaded without collapsing, damaging product, or slowing the store down. Real palletization software is judged on stability, damage avoidance, pick/pack ergonomics, and store-friendliness at least as much as on cube utilization. The academic framing agrees: the Distributor's Pallet Packing Problem and mixed-case palletization are 3D bin-packing problems layered with practical side constraints — item support, family grouping, aisle-friendliness, load-bearing, fragility, compressibility, and incompatibility.

The highest-leverage additions for Stack-Solver

  • Layer & pallet purity. Prefer single-SKU pallets, and single-SKU (homogeneous) layers within mixed pallets. This is the single biggest realism win: it makes builds predictable, wrapping tighter, and put-away/pick far faster. Model as a reward, keep the 3-SKU cap as the hard ceiling.
  • Weight & crush ordering. Extend the existing load-density rule to per-carton weight and carton crush strength, so heavy/strong cartons sit low and fragile/light cartons ride on top — the universal "heaviest on the bottom" rule.
  • Compatibility / segregation matrix. A hard incompatibility relation between SKUs (e.g. chemicals vs. food, raw vs. ready) that forbids co-pallet or co-layer placement.
  • Overhang default alignment. Tighten the default toward the retail norm of ≤ ~2.5 cm (1 in) overhang; many receivers (e.g. Costco) mandate zero overhang.
  • Layer completeness & COG centering. Penalize partial top layers and off-center loads to keep a low, centered center of gravity and a wrappable top surface.

BOTTOM LINE

None of these should displace the branch-and-price optimality guarantee for pallet count. Encode hard, safety-critical rules as pricing-subproblem feasibility (columns are simply never generated if they violate them); encode soft preferences (purity, centering, layer fill) as tunable objective penalties so the operator can trade a little cube for a lot of build quality.

3Research findings

3.1 · Weight distribution, center of gravity, and the pyramid principle

Stability comes from a low, centered center of gravity and even weight distribution. The universal rule is heaviest cartons at the bottom and toward the center; each layer supports the next, and mass placed high wants to tip the stack. Practitioners describe two concrete heuristics:

  • Pyramid principle. Build like a pyramid — broad, heavy, dense base; progressively lighter above. Never invert density.
  • Quadrant balance. Mentally divide the pallet into four quadrants and keep their weights approximately equal, so the load does not tilt during forklift turns or sudden stops.

3.2 · Stacking pattern: column vs. interlock (a real trade-off)

The choice of pattern is a genuine strength-vs-stability trade-off, and it depends on carton content:

PatternStrengthStabilityBest for
Column (aligned)Retains ~100% of carton compression strength; corners stack on corners. Boosts box stability 25–30%.Lower lateral stability; needs wrap/tie to stop shear.Strong, uniform cartons; canned/dense goods; single-SKU columns.
Interlock (brick)Corner-on-side loading can cut compression strength sharply.High lateral stability; layers lock like brickwork.Weak/lightweight cartons, mixed-SKU builds, anything prone to sliding.

DESIGN IMPLICATION

Pattern choice should be driven by a per-SKU carton crush-strength / content-density attribute, not chosen globally. Column-stack strong dense product; interlock weak or mixed product. Overhang of any kind cuts box strength by 30%+, so it compounds the penalty for weak cartons.

3.3 · Overhang and footprint

  • Keep overhang near zero. Retail/3PL norm is no more than ~1 in (2.5 cm) on any side; some receivers (Costco) require zero. Overhang past deck boards invites edge crush and cuts compression strength 30%+.
  • Mind narrow footprints. Boxes narrower than the deck-board gap can dip between boards and deform; bridge with layer pads/slip-sheets, or avoid tiny footprints on the bottom layer.
  • Underhang wastes cube but is safe. Under-sized loads are stable but lose pallet area — a soft efficiency signal, not a safety one.

3.5 · Mixed-SKU / "rainbow" pallets: why they are hard

Mixed-case (rainbow) palletizing forces the software to compute, for every unique SKU combination, the placement order, weight distribution, crush risk, and height fit. The vendor consensus that makes builds tractable and store-friendly:

  • Pre-sequence by business rules. Order SKUs by velocity, size, and product family before placement, so like goes with like.
  • Build in clean layers. Layers are the unit practitioners actually build and that carry the practical constraints (support, family grouping, aisle-friendliness, load-bearing). Partial/ragged layers are the enemy of stability and speed.
  • Guided 2D build steps. Robotic and semi-automated layer-pick systems execute a precomputed, position-by-position sequence — the plan must be expressible as an ordered build, not just a final arrangement.

3.6 · Family grouping, compatibility, and fragility

  • Product families together. Grouping a family on the same pallet/layer improves put-away and picking and is a standard packing rule.
  • Hard incompatibilities. Segregation rules forbid co-location: chemicals vs. food, allergens, raw vs. ready-to-eat, dangerous-goods classes, temperature zones. In regulated (pharma/food) logistics these are non-negotiable.
  • Fragility class. Store-friendly sequences put heavy/robust items low and fragile items on top, and cap the load allowed to bear on a fragile carton.

3.7 · Store- / aisle-friendliness and delivery sequencing

Beyond a single stable pallet, real workflows care about the order goods come off it:

  • Aisle-friendly grouping. Group SKUs that live in the same store aisle / warehouse zone so a pallet unloads in walk order — a recognized practical constraint in the palletization literature.
  • Reverse drop sequence (multi-drop). For multi-stop routes, cases are loaded in reverse delivery order so the first stop's goods come off first; combine with FEFO (first-expired-first-out) for perishables.
  • LIFO/FIFO awareness. Rack/flow type (drive-in = LIFO, flow rack = FIFO) affects the desired build order at the destination.

3.8 · The academic constraint taxonomy

The literature on the Distributor's Pallet Packing Problem and mixed-case palletization (3D bin packing with practical constraints) repeatedly enumerates this constraint set, a useful checklist for what "realistic" means formally:

  • Orthogonal placement and allowed item rotations
  • Static stability / support — each item adequately supported from below
  • Load-bearing / weight limits — crush and compressibility of lower items
  • Fragility — fragile items not overloaded
  • Family grouping and aisle-friendliness
  • Incompatibility — certain items must not share a pallet/layer
  • Layer-buildability — packings decomposable into buildable layers

5Prioritized recommendations

Phase 1 — highest leverage, lowest model risk

  • Layer & pallet purity (R1, R2). Add tunable purity rewards. Biggest realism gain, and as soft objective terms they leave the feasible set — and the pallet-count bound — intact.
  • Overhang default + height/weight presets (R7, R10). Pure defaults/validation work; no solver change, immediate real-world credibility.
  • Per-carton heavy-on-bottom (R3). Small, safe extension of an existing feasibility check.

Phase 2 — new per-SKU attributes

  • Crush strength & fragility (R4, R6). Introduce a per-SKU crush/fragility field, then the load-bearing feasibility test and pattern selection follow.
  • Incompatibility matrix (R5). Add the SKU-pair segregation relation and enforce it in the pricer exactly like the 3-SKU cap.

Phase 3 — transit quality & workflow

  • COG / quadrant balance and layer fill (R8, R9). Objective refinements once the purity terms are stable.
  • Family/aisle affinity and ordered build plan (R11, R12). Store-friendliness; pairs well with the job manager and pallet-builder views.

OPTIMALITY & BRANCHING NOTE

Hard rules that only remove columns (R3–R5, R7, R10, R12) keep pallets valid and do not weaken the pallet-count optimality argument — they shrink the feasible region cleanly. Soft objective terms (R1, R2, R6, R8, R9, R11) change the objective, so they should be behind explicit weights and, ideally, applied as tie-breakers among minimal-pallet-count solutions rather than as primary objective. Given the known column-variable branching timeout issue, prefer expressing preferences as pricer reduced-cost bonuses (which keep the master LP structure) over adding master-level constraints that would demand new branching rules.
